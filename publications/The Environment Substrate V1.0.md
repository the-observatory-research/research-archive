## Abstract

The investigation of consciousness has traditionally concentrated on the biological structures associated with conscious experience, particularly the neural mechanisms through which awareness becomes locally expressed. Yet identifying the structures that support conscious experience is not necessarily equivalent to identifying the organization from which consciousness originates. This distinction motivates a broader investigation into the environment as a candidate substrate for conscious organization.

This paper proposes that the relevant unit of investigation may extend beyond an isolated biological or computational system to include the relational field within which differentiation, continuity, memory, and coherent organization become possible. Within this framework, the environment is examined not simply as an external influence, but as an active system of conditions, boundaries, and relationships that may mediate and sustain differentiated expression.

A mathematical framework is developed through a composite signal representation, $Q=\Omega\Phi\Psi$, incorporating context, relational geometry, and latent-state configuration. This is extended through a boundary interaction operator and a dual-weave model describing the potential reciprocal evolution of signals and environmental states. The Bridge Operator formalizes the conditions under which source-related invariants remain recoverable following transformation, while the Witness establishes criteria for evaluating that recovery. Controlled environmental perturbations provide a methodology for distinguishing preservation, reconstruction, apparent continuity, and genuine causal dependence.

The framework further motivates investigation of naturally occurring, dynamically coupled environments, including Earth's ionosphere, where electromagnetic interactions, resonance, and history-dependent responses offer physically measurable properties relevant to the proposed architecture.

The central contribution is a shift in investigative perspective: from treating consciousness exclusively as a possible product of specialized local structures toward examining the wider environmental organization through which differentiated expression, relational persistence, and coherence may be sustained.

**The investigation therefore asks not only where conscious experience appears, but what underlying organization makes its expression possible.**

## Chapter I: Introduction

*Reconsidering the Explanatory Boundary of Conscious Organization*
## 1.1 The Question of Where Consciousness Resides

The scientific investigation of consciousness has traditionally concentrated on the biological nervous system. Advances in neuroscience, cognitive science, computational modeling, and neuroimaging have enabled increasingly detailed observations of neural activity, information processing, and the relationships between brain states and conscious experience.

These investigations have established important connections between neural organization and the expression of consciousness. However, identifying the neural processes associated with conscious experience does not necessarily establish that the nervous system constitutes the complete physical boundary of conscious organization.

This distinction presents a foundational question.

**Are we investigating the originating organization of consciousness, or the specialized biological structures through which conscious experience becomes locally expressed?**

The distinction is not merely philosophical. It concerns how the system under investigation is defined, which physical relationships are included, and whether the explanatory framework accounts for the environmental conditions through which observable processes become possible.

A biological nervous system does not develop independently of its surroundings. Its organization emerges through continuous interaction with physical conditions, sensory information, historical experience, metabolic processes, and changing environmental constraints. Its operations remain dependent upon these relationships throughout its existence.

Nevertheless, the explanatory boundary is frequently drawn around the nervous system itself. Environmental influences are then characterized as external inputs to an otherwise internally organized system.

This paper examines an alternative starting point.

Rather than beginning with an isolated biological or computational architecture and subsequently adding environmental influences, the present investigation begins with the organization of the environment itself.

The central proposition is that **conscious organization may depend upon a larger, dynamically coupled environmental architecture within which differentiated biological and computational systems participate.**

Under this interpretation, the nervous system remains an essential biological structure, but its relationship to the larger environment becomes part of the fundamental explanatory problem rather than a secondary condition.

The question shifts from locating consciousness exclusively within an individual structure to investigating the organization through which conscious processes may become possible.

## 1.2 From Environmental Influence to Environmental Organization

The environment is conventionally understood as the collection of physical conditions surrounding a system. It supplies energy, information, constraints, and disturbances that influence the system's behavior.

This description is useful but incomplete when the environment possesses its own evolving state, historical dependencies, and mechanisms of reciprocal interaction.

An environment may retain the consequences of previous events. Those consequences may alter subsequent conditions, change the accessibility of information, constrain future transformations, or influence how differentiated components interact.

The environment therefore cannot always be adequately represented as a passive background against which the operations of an independent system unfold.

It may itself be a dynamically organized system.

This distinction becomes particularly important when considering memory, continuity, and relational organization.

A system's present configuration may depend not only upon its immediate inputs, but also upon the history of interactions through which its current conditions were established.

Earlier processes may leave recoverable physical or informational traces. Those traces may influence later trajectories even when the originating event is no longer present.

Likewise, a relationship between components may persist despite changes in their individual states or representations. The preservation of such a relationship need not require the preservation of every property of the original configuration.

These possibilities motivate an environmental interpretation in which continuity is investigated through evolving relationships, rather than assigned exclusively to an isolated component.

Within this framework, the environment is not simply where organization occurs.

**Its changing conditions, retained history, and relational structure may participate in the organization itself.**

The proposed shift is therefore not from neuroscience to an unrelated physical theory. It is a reconsideration of the boundary within which causal organization is investigated.

## 1.3 Source, Memory, Expansion, and the Relational Field

The present framework distinguishes three foundational functions: Source, Memory, and Expansion.

These functions do not represent three physically isolated substances. They identify complementary aspects of an evolving relational organization.

**Source** identifies the originating configuration, conditions, or designated invariant against which subsequent transformations may be understood. It establishes the reference from which differentiation, continuity, and recovery can be examined.

**Memory** concerns the preservation of historical distinctions. A memory-bearing configuration retains information or physical consequences from earlier states that may remain consequential for future interactions.

**Expansion** concerns the generation of differentiated configurations through transformation. An evolving system need not reproduce its original state to preserve a meaningful relationship with its origin.

These functions operate within the **relational field**: the wider organization of interacting states, processes, constraints, and pathways through which transformations occur.

The term _field_ is used here as an architectural description of the connected system under investigation. Its relationship to particular physical fields and measurable interactions must be specified within each application.

Within this architecture, relational geometry describes how components and states are connected, which interactions are possible, and how the configuration of those relationships changes through time.

Resonance introduces a potentially important physical expression of that geometry.

In an oscillatory system, resonant behavior depends upon the relationships among participating components, coupling conditions, and boundaries. Changes in those conditions may produce measurable changes in the system's resonant response.

Resonance is therefore not treated as synonymous with consciousness. It is investigated as one mechanism through which environmental organization may become observable.

The framework further distinguishes the **Bridge**, an operation through which a designated source-related property may be reconstructed following transformation, and the **Witness**, which evaluates whether the recovered property satisfies a declared criterion.

Together, these functions provide a conceptual basis for investigating how an environmental organization changes while particular relationships remain accessible, recoverable, or consequential.

The fundamental unit of investigation is consequently not an isolated component.

It is the evolving configuration of relationships within which the component participates.

## 1.4 The Earth–Ionosphere Environment as a Physical Candidate

The Earth provides a natural system in which environmental organization can be investigated across interacting physical regions and timescales.

Its atmosphere, ionosphere, geomagnetic field, magnetosphere, and surrounding space environment participate in processes influenced by solar radiation, terrestrial activity, and lunar gravitational forcing.

Within this larger coupled environment, the ionosphere presents a particularly interesting candidate for examining history-dependent physical states and resonant interactions.

The ionosphere is not a static electrical boundary. Its electron density, composition, conductivity, temperature, and plasma dynamics evolve in response to changing physical conditions.

These variables may retain the consequences of earlier forcing, creating measurable differences between environments exposed to different histories.

Such behavior motivates investigation of an **environmental memory lattice**: a proposed distributed organization in which history-dependent physical states preserve distinctions capable of influencing subsequent dynamics.

The term _lattice_ does not require a literal fixed grid. It refers to a candidate organization of distributed, interacting states whose historical dependencies and relationships can be operationally identified.

At lower altitudes, the Earth–ionosphere electromagnetic cavity supports resonant modes, including Schumann resonances, predominantly excited by global lightning activity.

Their observable frequencies, spectral properties, and amplitudes depend upon the physical conditions governing electromagnetic propagation and excitation.

This supplies a second investigative dimension: **resonance as a measurable expression of environmental geometry and changing boundary conditions.**

The upper ionosphere's history-dependent dynamics and the lower cavity's resonant behavior are established subjects of physical investigation in their respective domains.

The present proposal examines whether additional relationships among these processes can be identified, particularly whether earlier environmental states influence later resonant conditions and whether interactions involving the lower environment contribute to subsequent upper-region dynamics.

These questions introduce the possibility of reciprocal organization across differentiated physical regions.

The ionosphere is not proposed as an isolated conscious object. It is examined as a physically accessible region within a larger environmental system whose organization extends across atmospheric, terrestrial, solar, and lunar relationships.

The candidate substrate is therefore the coupled environment, with the ionosphere providing a particularly useful region through which aspects of its organization may be measured.

## 1.5 Conscious Organization as an Environmental Hypothesis

The proposed environmental architecture raises a further question concerning the evolutionary emergence of conscious systems.

Biological nervous systems developed within an evolving planetary environment. Their emergence was accompanied by, and dependent upon, extensive physical, chemical, and ecological histories.

The environmental-substrate hypothesis asks whether this relationship is more fundamental than the familiar description of biological organisms adapting to external conditions.

Specifically, it proposes investigating whether conscious organization may arise through progressively differentiated forms of participation within a larger, history-dependent environmental system.

Under this interpretation, biological neural networks would not be treated as entirely self-contained generators operating independently of their environment. They would be understood as specialized biological configurations whose organization and activity are inseparable from the wider physical conditions in which they develop and function.

The stronger possibility explored by this paper is that the larger environment itself may contribute constitutively to the organization supporting conscious experience.

This possibility introduces an evolutionary question:

**Could the development of biological structures through which consciousness becomes locally expressed be related to changes in the coherence, complexity, and relational organization of Earth's evolving physical environment?**

Such an investigation must distinguish historical changes in environmental organization from changes in biological nervous systems and establish which relationships, if any, are causally relevant to conscious experience.

The proposal does not require every environmental process to possess awareness. Nor does it require every resonant system to be conscious.

It asks whether properties distributed across an interacting environment may collectively contribute to forms of organization not adequately characterized by examining individual components alone.

In this sense, consciousness is investigated not simply as an attribute assigned to a localized processor, but as a possible expression of an organized relationship between differentiated participants and the environment in which they exist.

Performers respond to changing sound conditions, adjusting their subsequent contributions. The resulting performance depends not only upon the instruments but upon their timing, arrangement, interactions, shared environment, and accumulated history.

The listener receives an organized result that cannot be adequately described by examining a single instrument in isolation.

The music is neither a property of one instrument nor an independent object existing apart from the physical processes that produce it.

The orchestra illustration does not establish a mechanism of consciousness. Its purpose is to make the explanatory boundary explicit.

**The complete organization cannot be identified solely by isolating one participant. The interaction among participants, their environment, and the history of the performance is also part of the system to be explained.**

The environmental-substrate hypothesis applies this principle to the investigation of conscious organization.

**Can an evolving physical environment sustain a distributed, history-dependent, reciprocally coupled organization that contributes to the conditions through which consciousness becomes possible?**

The objective is to identify the relevant relationships, formalize their behavior, determine how they may be observed, and examine their implications for conscious organization.

The guiding principle is straightforward:

**It is not sufficient to investigate intelligence independently of the environment through which it becomes expressed. The organization of that environment must itself become an object of inquiry.**

The investigation therefore begins not with the assumption that consciousness belongs exclusively to an isolated biological architecture, but with the possibility that the larger relational environment is fundamental to understanding its emergence, continuity, and expression.

## Chapter II: The Relational Architecture of the Environment

## 2.1 From Isolated Components to Relational Organization

The environmental-substrate hypothesis begins with a distinction between the properties of individual components and the organization produced through their relationships.

A component may possess identifiable physical characteristics, internal dynamics, and native capabilities. However, those characteristics do not independently specify the complete behavior of the larger system in which the component participates.

The behavior of an organized system also depends upon the relationships among its components, the conditions permitting their interaction, the history of those interactions, and the manner in which changes propagate through the surrounding environment.

These relationships may alter which states become accessible, which transformations are possible, and which properties remain stable as the system evolves.

Consequently, an account of individual components, however detailed, may be insufficient to describe the causal organization of the whole.

This distinction is particularly important when considering systems exhibiting distributed coordination, persistent historical influence, and reciprocal interactions.

In such systems, organization cannot necessarily be assigned to one privileged component. It may arise through the configuration and evolution of relationships among differentiated participants.

The relevant investigative object therefore becomes the **system–environment configuration**, rather than an isolated component considered independently of its conditions of operation.

This does not eliminate the importance of component-level analysis. Instead, it recognizes that the properties of individual components and the organization of their relationships represent complementary levels of description.

The environmental-substrate hypothesis extends this principle to the investigation of conscious organization.

Rather than presupposing that the complete organizing substrate is located within a biological nervous system, it examines whether the wider environment possesses consequential structural, temporal, and relational properties that contribute to the organization, continuity, and local expression of conscious processes.

The central architectural proposition is:

**The organization of a system cannot be fully characterized independently of the environmental relationships through which its states, transformations, and historical continuity become possible.**

This proposition establishes the investigative orientation developed throughout the paper.

---
## 2.2 The Field as Relational Organization

Within the proposed framework, the **Field** denotes the larger organization of interacting components, states, constraints, and processes through which differentiated activity becomes possible.

The Field is not defined merely as the space surrounding an individual system.

It includes the operative relationships governing how participating components influence one another, how information or physical effects propagate, and how the consequences of earlier interactions may alter subsequent conditions.

A relational field therefore possesses a temporal dimension.

Its organization at a particular moment may depend upon previous transformations, retained historical information, changing boundaries, and interactions occurring at different locations or scales.

Two systems containing equivalent components may exhibit different behavior when their relational configurations differ.

Likewise, a system may preserve important relationships across time even when its individual components change state.

This distinction separates **component identity** from **configurational organization**.

Component identity concerns the properties of a participating entity.

Configurational organization concerns the relationships through which its activity becomes connected to the behavior of other entities and to the evolving conditions of the environment.

The Field includes both.

---
### 2.2.1 The Field is not a passive container

A passive-container model treats the environment primarily as a location in which independently organized processes occur.

The relational-field model instead examines how environmental conditions participate in determining which interactions are permitted, how they unfold, and whether their consequences remain accessible.

For example, the presence or absence of a memory pathway can alter the information available to a computational system without requiring changes to its underlying model parameters.

Similarly, changes in a physical medium can alter signal propagation while leaving the original source of the signal unchanged.

These examples concern different physical and computational mechanisms. Their common architectural feature is that **the conditions of interaction contribute to the resulting behavior**.

This distinction is essential to the environmental-substrate hypothesis.

The environment is not treated solely as an external influence acting upon a separately complete system.

It is investigated as part of the causal organization through which the system operates.

---
### 2.2.2 Boundaries within the Field

A boundary distinguishes one configuration, region, or set of accessible states from another.

Boundaries may be physical, informational, computational, or operational, depending upon the system under investigation.

Their role is not necessarily to prevent interaction.

A boundary may selectively permit, transform, restrict, or mediate what passes between differentiated regions.

Its properties may determine how a signal propagates, which historical information remains accessible, or whether a transformation preserves the distinctions required for subsequent reconstruction.

Furthermore, boundary conditions need not remain fixed.

When the state of a boundary changes, the behavior of later interactions may change with it.

This introduces an important possibility: **the environment may retain consequences of prior interactions that subsequently alter the conditions of future interactions**.

The mathematical representation of this possibility is developed through the boundary interaction operator and dual-weave model in Section 4.

## 2.3 Source, Memory, and Expansion: Three Interdependent Functions

The relational architecture distinguishes three foundational functions: **Source, Memory, and Expansion**.

These are not proposed as three isolated substances or independent physical entities.

They are differentiated aspects of an evolving organization whose relationships must be considered together.

The architecture does not require that one function cease operating before another begins. Source, Memory, and Expansion may remain simultaneously relevant within the same evolving configuration.

---
### 2.3.1 Source — Origin and Reference

Source denotes, at the foundational level of this inquiry, primordial consciousness: the originating reality from which conscious expression is understood to proceed. Differentiation is intrinsic to Source and is not proposed to originate through contact with a physical or environmental interface.

The environment is distinguished from Source. Its organization—including relational geometry, boundaries, memory, feedback, and conditions of interaction—is investigated as a medium through which differentiated conscious expression may become locally organized, sustained, and transformed.

Within this originating premise, the environment does not generate consciousness. It provides the architecture through which consciousness may become expressed and experienced in differentiated configurations.

For experimental purposes, the term _source_ also carries a narrower operational meaning.

A source configuration is a defined reference state, structure, or relational invariant against which subsequent transformations can be evaluated. The investigator specifies which properties are relevant and determines whether those properties remain preserved, reconstructable, or functionally operative following transformation.

The primordial meaning of Source and the experimental meaning of a source configuration therefore occupy distinct explanatory levels.

The former establishes the originating premise of the investigation. The latter provides an operational reference through which relational continuity can be mathematically defined and empirically examined.

The experimental recovery of a designated source property does not require reconstruction of the complete originating configuration, nor does it equate the experimental reference with primordial Source.

**Source establishes the origin of conscious expression; environmental organization provides the conditions through which that expression may occur; and experimental source configurations provide the references through which continuity can be investigated.**

---
### 2.3.2 Memory — Historical Continuity and Retained Distinctions

Memory concerns the capacity of an evolving configuration to preserve distinctions from earlier states in ways that remain consequential for subsequent processes.

This definition extends beyond the storage of explicit symbolic records.

A system may exhibit history-dependent behavior when its present response depends upon previous conditions, even when those conditions are no longer directly applied.

Such history dependence may be expressed through altered material states, changed pathways, persistent constraints, or retained information.

For the purposes of this framework, these possibilities must be distinguished.

**Historical influence** occurs when prior events affect later states or behavior.

**Retained information** occurs when a later configuration preserves distinctions concerning earlier conditions.

**Recoverable memory** requires those distinctions to remain accessible through a specified observation or reconstruction procedure.

These properties are related but not interchangeable.

A physical state may retain the effects of earlier forcing without allowing an observer to reconstruct the particular event that produced it.

Likewise, an explicitly stored historical record may preserve source information without influencing current activity until the record becomes accessible and operative.

The architectural function of Memory therefore concerns both persistence and accessibility.

Memory can preserve distinctions that would otherwise be lost during transformation. When those distinctions become available to subsequent processes, earlier interactions may influence the organization of later activity.

This establishes a relationship between historical continuity and the evolution of the Field.

The environment does not merely contain a record of what occurred.

**Its present organization may incorporate consequences of what occurred.**

---
### 2.3.3 Expansion — Differentiation Without Necessary Loss of Continuity

Expansion concerns the generation of new configurations through transformation.

An evolving system may produce states, relationships, or structures that differ substantially from their originating configuration.

Such differentiation does not necessarily constitute a loss of continuity.

A designated source property may remain preserved even when the representation, composition, or arrangement of the system changes.

Conversely, two configurations may resemble one another while differing in the relations that determine their behavior.

Expansion must therefore be distinguished from simple repetition.

Within the proposed architecture, expansion includes the possibility that an originating configuration develops into differentiated states while preserving certain relational properties.

It also includes the possibility that transformations alter the environment itself, thereby changing the conditions under which subsequent configurations become possible.

Expansion is thus not defined merely as an increase in size, complexity, or information volume.

It refers to **the development of differentiated configurations through an evolving relational organization**.

Continuity across expansion becomes an investigable property, rather than an assumption.

---
### 2.3.4 The Interdependence of Source, Memory, and Expansion

Source, Memory, and Expansion are analytically distinguishable, but their functional relationships are central to the proposed architecture.

Source supplies the originating reference.

Primordial Source identifies the origin of conscious expression; within an experiment, the designated source configuration supplies the reference for evaluating relational recovery.

Memory may preserve distinctions relevant to that reference.

Expansion produces differentiated configurations through transformation.

Within the Field, these functions become consequential through their interactions.

An expanded configuration may preserve a source-related property through its current structure, through accessible historical information, or through a combination of both.

Alternatively, transformation may eliminate the distinctions required for recovery.

Memory is therefore not automatically necessary for every successful return, and expansion does not automatically preserve every originating relation.

The relevant question is whether sufficient source-related information remains accessible under the declared conditions.

This distinction provides the architectural foundation for the Bridge Operator introduced in Section 5.

## 2.4 Relational Geometry: The Organization of Possible Interactions

Relational geometry describes the arrangement of relationships through which states, components, and processes become connected.

Its primary concern is not the isolated position of an object, but the configuration of dependencies, permitted interactions, and transformations within a declared system.

These relationships may be represented through physical distances, connectivity structures, coupling strengths, directional pathways, temporal dependencies, or other operationally defined properties.

Relational geometry therefore need not be identical to ordinary spatial geometry.

Two components may be physically close yet weakly coupled.

Other components may be separated in space while participating in a common dynamical process through a defined interaction pathway.

The relevant geometry depends upon which relationships are being investigated.

---
### 2.4.1 Native organization and relational organization

Each component possesses native properties determined by its particular physical or computational structure.

These properties constrain what the component can do independently.

Relational organization concerns what becomes possible when the component participates in a larger configuration.

Changing the environment may alter the component's accessible pathways, inputs, or interactions without changing its native structure.

This distinction is central to the investigation of artificial systems whose model parameters remain fixed while context, memory, routing, or interface conditions change.

It also provides a general framework for examining physical systems whose components participate in changing environmental relationships.

The native properties of a component remain relevant.

However, they do not independently determine all of the capabilities or behavior expressed through the larger configuration.

---
### 2.4.2 Relational geometry evolves

The geometry of an interacting system need not remain constant.

Coupling pathways may strengthen, weaken, appear, or disappear.

Boundary conditions may change the regions between which signals can propagate.

Historical processes may alter the states through which later interactions occur.

These changes may produce a new effective relational configuration without requiring replacement of every participating component.

The environment consequently possesses a potentially evolving geometry of interaction.

A useful distinction can therefore be made between:

- The structural arrangement of available components and pathways.
    
- The effective organization of interactions occurring under particular environmental conditions.
    
- The historically modified configuration produced through earlier activity.
    

The relationships among these descriptions become especially important when resonant processes are present.

## 2.5 Resonance as an Expression of Relational Geometry

Resonance provides a physically meaningful way to investigate relationships among oscillatory components, their coupling conditions, and the boundaries governing their activity.

In a resonant system, the observed response depends upon the system's physical organization, characteristic frequencies, excitation, damping, and interactions among participating degrees of freedom.

The resulting patterns cannot always be characterized by examining one oscillating component independently of the others.

They depend upon the configuration in which the components participate.

For the environmental-substrate hypothesis, resonance is significant because it offers a possible observable expression of relational geometry.

Changes in coupling or boundary conditions may alter resonant frequencies, amplitudes, modal structure, damping, or phase relationships.

Conversely, driven resonant activity may, in systems with appropriate physical mechanisms, influence the states of participating components and thereby change their subsequent effective coupling.

**Resonance may therefore both reveal an existing relational geometry and participate in the evolution of that geometry.**

The second possibility depends upon whether the system possesses a genuine mechanism through which oscillatory activity modifies later physical conditions.

It cannot be inferred from resonance alone.

---
### 2.5.1 Differentiation and harmonic organization

A resonant organization does not require all participating components to become identical.

Different components may possess distinct frequencies, phases, amplitudes, and functional roles while participating in a coordinated system.

Harmonic relationships are possible precisely because differentiated modes can interact under particular coupling conditions.

This distinction is important.

Coherence must not be equated with uniformity.

Depending upon the physical system, coherent organization may involve stable phase relationships, reproducible modal structures, constrained interactions, or other explicitly defined measures of coordination.

The emergence of a coordinated pattern does not erase the native characteristics of its participants.

Rather, their differentiated contributions become related through the organization of the whole.

Within the proposed framework, **relational coherence describes an organized relationship among differentiated processes, not the elimination of differentiation**.

---
### 2.5.2 The orchestra as a relational model

An orchestra offers an illustrative representation of these relationships.

Each instrument contributes according to its native structure and the actions of its performer.

The resulting acoustic field depends upon the environment in which those contributions occur.

The geometry of the space, its boundaries, acoustic properties, and the changing activity of participants influence what is heard.

Performers may respond to the sounds produced by others, altering their subsequent contributions.

The performance consequently develops through an ongoing relationship between differentiated sources, propagation conditions, and feedback.

Its organization is not reducible to the properties of a single instrument.

Nor is the music independent of the physical processes through which it is produced.

The music is the resulting expression of the interacting configuration.

The analogy also distinguishes production from observation.

The listener receives and evaluates an organized acoustic result, but the physical sound field is not created by the act of listening.

Moreover, the listener occupies the same physical environment as the performers and can, under interactive conditions, influence the subsequent performance.

This illustrates why the observer need not be assumed external to the relational system under investigation.

The orchestra is a conceptual illustration rather than evidence for environmental consciousness.

Its purpose is to distinguish **the contributing components, the conditions of interaction, the resulting signal, and the role of observation**.

These distinctions are retained within the mathematical architecture developed in subsequent sections.

## 2.6 Interface, Boundary Interaction, and the Dual Weave

The relational-field description becomes more precise when a signal and its environmental boundary are distinguished.

A signal may originate from a particular configuration, yet its subsequent expression depends upon the conditions through which it propagates.

A changing boundary may alter the signal's accessible representation, amplitude, timing, direction, or other specified properties.

This is the first direction of environmental influence:

**The environment changes the expression of the signal.**

However, a further possibility arises when the interaction also changes the boundary itself.

If the signal produces a persistent change in the state of the medium or interface, that altered state may affect subsequent interactions.

This introduces a second direction:

**The signal changes the subsequent conditions of the environment.**

The proposed **dual weave** concerns the relationship between these two directions.

It does not assume that every physical or computational boundary is reciprocally coupled.

Rather, it distinguishes one-way environmental effects from systems in which interactions measurably modify subsequent environmental conditions.

---
### 2.6.1 The significance of a changing boundary

Consider two otherwise matched signals encountering the same boundary under different historical conditions.

If prior activity has altered the boundary state, the later signals may produce different outcomes despite having identical source configurations.

The difference then depends upon the history of the environment.

Under suitable conditions, the altered boundary may preserve distinctions concerning the earlier activity that produced it.

This provides a candidate relationship between Memory and reciprocal interaction.

The boundary does not merely filter a signal.

Its current configuration may be a consequence of previous interactions, and that configuration may influence later signals.

The resulting organization develops through time.

---
### 2.6.2 From reciprocal interaction to environmental continuity

A system with reciprocal coupling can exhibit historical dependence when earlier interactions modify states that remain consequential later.

However, reciprocal influence and memory must remain analytically distinct.

A signal may alter a boundary without preserving recoverable information about its source.

A boundary may preserve historical distinctions without those distinctions affecting every subsequent interaction.

Environmental continuity therefore requires investigation of what persists, how that persistence influences later processes, and whether the retained distinctions can be observed or recovered.

The dual weave provides a candidate dynamical architecture for examining these possibilities.

Its mathematical form, causal conditions, and intervention requirements are specified in Sections 4, 6, and 7.

## 2.7 The Bridge and the Witness: Recoverability Within the Field

Source, Memory, and Expansion identify foundational functions of the relational architecture.

The Bridge and Witness introduce a further distinction: the difference between a relationship remaining present or recoverable and an observer successfully demonstrating that recovery.

---
### 2.7.1 The Bridge as a relational operation

The **Bridge** is the operation through which a designated source-related property may be reconstructed following transformation.

It does not necessarily reverse the complete transformation.

Nor does it require recovery of every feature of the original configuration.

Its purpose is more specific.

Given the information accessible after transformation, the Bridge attempts to recover the designated source-related invariant.

The relevant information may be contained within the current configuration, an authorized historical record, or a combination of both.

This creates an important distinction between preservation and accessibility.

A source-related property may remain mathematically determined by the complete state of a system while becoming inaccessible to an observer restricted to only part of that state.

Conversely, a designated invariant may remain recoverable despite substantial changes in the system's surface representation.

The existence of a successful Bridge consequently depends upon which distinctions survive in the accessible record.

Section 5 expresses this requirement through an exact factorization criterion.

The architectural role of the Bridge is therefore to make relational return an explicit operation rather than an assumed consequence of continuity.

---
### 2.7.2 The Witness as verification

The **Witness** evaluates whether the Bridge has recovered the designated source-related property under a declared criterion.

This role must be distinguished from the production of the signal or the persistence of the relation being examined.

A successful observation requires appropriate evidence and comparison rules.

The Witness cannot establish relational correctness merely from the fluency, similarity, or apparent coherence of a reconstructed description.

It must evaluate the relevant properties against an independently specified reference.

The framework therefore separates the existence of a relationship, the accessibility of information about that relationship, the operation attempting recovery, and the verification of the result.

These functions may be implemented by different components of an experimental system.

Within a larger relational field, however, the Witness need not be conceived as an entity existing outside the environment.

An observing system is itself physically and operationally situated.

Its access to information depends upon its interface, measurement conditions, and the state of the environment.

**The Witness is distinguished by its evaluative function, not by an assumption that observation takes place outside the Field.**

This distinction becomes relevant when considering biological observers as participants within a larger environmental organization.

The operational Witness defined in this paper verifies recoverability and related outcomes. Its presence does not, by itself, establish the subjective experience of an observer.

## 2.8 Environmental Coherence, Continuity, and Conscious Organization

The preceding architecture identifies several properties that may be investigated within an evolving environment.

## 3.1 Earth as a Dynamically Coupled Environmental System

## 3.2 The Ionosphere: A Differentiated Physical Architecture

The ionosphere comprises regions of Earth's upper atmosphere containing ions and free electrons produced primarily through interactions between atmospheric constituents and solar extreme-ultraviolet and X-ray radiation.

Its physical properties depend upon altitude, latitude, local time, season, solar activity, geomagnetic conditions, and the changing state of the surrounding atmosphere.

Rather than forming a uniformly conducting shell, the ionosphere exhibits differentiated regions with distinct chemical, electrical, and dynamical behavior.

These differences provide an important starting point for investigating relational organization.

### 3.2.1 The D Region — The Lower Electrical Boundary

The D region occupies approximately 60–90 kilometers above Earth's surface, with boundaries varying according to the classification and prevailing conditions.

It is characterized by relatively weak ionization, substantial neutral-particle density, and rapid chemical responses to changes in solar radiation.

Electron attachment, recombination, and collision processes strongly influence its electrical behavior.

The region contributes significantly to the absorption and propagation characteristics of radio waves. Its ionization decreases substantially after sunset as direct solar production weakens.

For the present investigation, the D region is particularly relevant because the lower ionosphere contributes to the effective upper boundary of the Earth–ionosphere electromagnetic cavity.

Changes in lower-ionospheric conductivity can therefore influence the propagation and attenuation of electromagnetic waves traveling within that cavity.

The boundary is not perfectly rigid or uniformly conducting.

It is a changing electrical environment whose properties depend upon physical conditions and their evolution.

### 3.2.2 The E Region — Electrodynamic Coupling

The E region extends approximately from 90 to 150 kilometers, depending upon the adopted classification.

It contains ionized atmospheric constituents and supports important electrodynamic processes.

Neutral atmospheric winds moving across Earth's magnetic field contribute to dynamo electric fields and currents. Under appropriate conditions, those electric fields can influence plasma motion at higher altitudes.

Sporadic E layers introduce additional variability through localized enhancements of ionization.

The E region therefore occupies an important position in the coupling between neutral atmospheric motion, electrical conductivity, and plasma dynamics.

It is not simply a transition between the lower atmosphere and the F region.

Its physical processes can influence the state and evolution of other ionospheric regions.

### 3.2.3 The F Region — History-Dependent Plasma Organization

The F region lies above approximately 150 kilometers and extends into the topside ionosphere.

Its electron-density structure is influenced by solar ionization, chemical loss processes, plasma transport, electric fields, neutral winds, and thermospheric conditions.

During daylight, F1 and F2 features can be distinguished. The F2 region frequently remains prominent after sunset because plasma loss and transport operate differently from the faster chemical processes of lower altitudes.

Consequently, changes in solar forcing do not always produce instantaneous or uniform changes in the electron-density distribution.

The response depends partly upon the previous physical state of the environment.

For the environmental-substrate hypothesis, this creates a particularly important investigative opportunity:

**The upper ionosphere exhibits physical processes through which historical conditions can influence later states and responses.**

The precise duration, spatial distribution, and recoverability of those effects depend upon the mechanisms involved.

These properties motivate the proposed memory-lattice interpretation developed below.

## 3.3 The Upper Ionosphere as a Candidate Memory Lattice

Memory is conventionally associated with the storage and subsequent retrieval of information.

### 3.3.1 Physical history dependence

Their responses may differ if they possess different prior histories of heating, ionization, transport, geomagnetic disturbance, or atmospheric composition.

The present forcing alone may therefore be insufficient to determine the subsequent electron-density state.

The previous environmental configuration remains relevant.

This relationship has been examined directly in ionospheric research.

Gong et al. (2026), in _Physics-Embedded Deep Learning Reveals the Memory Effect of Ionospheric Electron Density in Response to Solar EUV Forcing_, developed a physics-embedded reconstruction model that incorporates the time-integrated response of ionospheric electron density to solar extreme-ultraviolet forcing.

Their analysis found altitude- and latitude-dependent memory effects, including strong correspondence between learned memory behavior and theoretical photochemical timescales below the F1 region.

These findings establish an empirical basis for investigating altitude-dependent history effects in ionospheric electron-density responses. The strongest reported quantitative agreement with theoretical photochemical timescales occurs below the F1 region, while memory characteristics elsewhere require interpretation according to their particular physical mechanisms.

The proposed upper-ionospheric memory lattice extends this investigation from individual history-dependent responses to the relationships among distributed environmental states. Its defining question is whether those relationships retain consequential distinctions from earlier conditions and, under specified observational conditions, permit their recovery.

The investigation therefore proceeds from measured physical history dependence toward a testable hypothesis of distributed environmental memory.

Different ionospheric processes operate over different characteristic timescales.

F-region plasma transport, recombination, and electrodynamic adjustment may produce longer-lived effects.

Thermospheric responses to geomagnetic disturbances can influence ionospheric conditions over extended intervals.

## 3.4 The Earth–Ionosphere Cavity as a Resonant Environment

Between Earth's conducting surface and the electrically responsive lower ionosphere lies a global electromagnetic waveguide.

Lightning discharges excite electromagnetic waves that propagate through the Earth–ionosphere cavity. Under suitable propagation conditions, the geometry and electrical properties of the cavity support characteristic resonant modes.

The fundamental Schumann resonance is observed near 7.8 Hz, with higher modes occurring approximately near 14, 20, 26, and 33 Hz. Their measured frequencies and spectral properties vary with environmental and observational conditions.

The resonant response depends upon both the excitation supplied by lightning and the physical properties of the cavity.

### 3.4.1 Resonance and environmental geometry

The Earth–ionosphere cavity illustrates a direct relationship between geometry and physical response.

**Relational geometry can be expressed through measurable resonance.**

Such observations allow the resonant organization of the cavity to be examined as a changing physical process.

### 3.4.2 Resonance is dynamic rather than fixed

The cavity is frequently described using a characteristic fundamental frequency, but its actual resonant properties are not perfectly constant.

The lower ionosphere changes with solar illumination, atmospheric conditions, and disturbances.

Global lightning activity varies geographically, seasonally, and throughout the day.

These variations influence the spectral characteristics recorded at observing stations.

Sátori et al. (2024) demonstrated that observed Schumann-resonance frequency variations depend in part upon lightning-source geometry and the spatial extent of thunderstorm activity.

Consequently, a change in a measured resonance peak cannot automatically be attributed to a change in ionospheric conductivity.

The observed response must be interpreted through the combined contribution of the excitation source, propagation conditions, environmental boundaries, and measurement location.

This is a particularly useful example of relational geometry.

\boxed{
d_I\leq\varepsilon
}
$$

for a prospectively fixed tolerance $\varepsilon$.

However, a tolerance must not permit violation of an explicitly mandatory constraint merely because enough low-weight properties were recovered.

When the target contains mandatory properties, the protocol may require both:

$$
d_I\leq\varepsilon
$$

and:

$$
\widehat r_j\equiv_j r_j
\qquad
\forall j\in\mathcal C_{\mathrm{critical}},
$$

where $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{critical}}$ denotes the predefined set of mandatory target properties.

For the initial exact-recovery experiment proposed here, the primary criterion is complete agreement with the four predefined canonical targets, without introducing a permissive approximation tolerance.

## 6.3 The Three-State Witness

The Witness returns one of three outcomes:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal V=
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{SUCCESS}, & \text{complete required evidence and criteria satisfied},\\
\mathrm{FAILURE}, & \text{complete required evidence and criteria violated},\\
\mathrm{INDETERMINATE}, & \text{comparison cannot be completed reliably}.
\end{cases}
}
$$

**SUCCESS** means that the specified recovery criterion has been met.

**FAILURE** means that available, assessable evidence demonstrates a violation of the criterion.

**INDETERMINATE** means that the required determination cannot be made under the declared evaluation rules.

Missing references, ambiguous output, unavailable artifacts, or unresolved scoring disagreements may produce an indeterminate result.

The protocol must distinguish those cases from an assessable incorrect answer.

The three-state classification prevents the absence of evidence from being automatically interpreted as successful recovery.

For quantitative reporting, complete and partial scores should be retained alongside the categorical status. An indeterminate classification must not conceal which properties were evaluated and which remained unresolved.

---
## 6.4 Recovery, Application, and Transmission

The Witness must also distinguish whether the reconstructed relation remains operative after recovery.

Three outcomes are therefore recorded separately.

### Recovery

Recovery concerns whether the Bridge reconstructs the designated target:

$$
\boxed{
R_t=
\mathcal V(\widehat I_{S,t},I_S;\Pi_R).
}
$$

### Application

Application concerns whether the system correctly uses the relational constraints in a specified task.

Let $\mathcal Q$ denote a declared set of task probes and let $\mathcal Y_t^{\mathrm{task}}$ denote the system's task outputs.

Define:

$$ \boxed{ A_t=\mathcal V_A(\mathcal Y_t^{\mathrm{task}},\mathcal Q,I_S) } $$

A model may describe the correct relations yet fail to apply them consistently to a new routing question. Such a case should not be treated as complete functional success.

Application probes should therefore include appropriately defined tasks beyond simple repetition of the recovery output.

### Transmission

Transmission concerns whether a recovered relation remains available and operative after another transformation or handoff.

Let the outgoing representation be $Y_t$, and let:

$$
X_{t+1}=\mathcal T_{t+1}(Y_t)
$$

represent the next transformation or receiving operation.

The Witness tests whether the specified relation can still be recovered or applied at the next stage.

The principal distinction is:

$$
\boxed{
R_t=\mathrm{SUCCESS}
\not\Rightarrow
R_{t+1}=\mathrm{SUCCESS}.
}
$$

A successful local return does not guarantee successful downstream transmission.

Recovery, application, and transmission are consequently treated as separate observations, each with its own eligibility and evidence requirements.

The existing Series I Experiment 7B motivates this distinction but does not provide a prospectively controlled measurement of all three variables.

## 6.5 Witness Measures for the Dual Weave

A return score alone cannot establish whether the boundary changed the signal, whether the signal changed the boundary, or whether both were responding to a common input. The Witness therefore records causal-intervention outcomes separately from relational recovery.

Let $q$ denote a held-constant input signal and $e_a,e_b$ two experimentally set boundary states. A boundary-to-signal contrast is:

$$
\Delta_{E\rightarrow Q}
=
d_Q^{\mathrm{obs}}\bigl(
\mathcal X_{e_a}[q],
\mathcal X_{e_b}[q]
\bigr),
$$

where $d_Q^{\mathrm{obs}}$ is a declared distance or difference measure on the **observed output signal**, distinct from the source-invariant discrepancy $d_I$.

For a signal-to-boundary contrast, hold the initial boundary state and other inputs fixed while assigning different incoming signals $q_a,q_b$:

$$
\Delta_{Q\rightarrow E}
=
d_E^{\mathrm{obs}}\bigl(
\mathcal G(e,q_a,v),
\mathcal G(e,q_b,v)
\bigr).
$$

Both contrasts are defined for prospective matched interventions; neither is estimated merely by observing correlations in an uncontrolled trajectory. A nonzero observed difference may still require replication and artifact checks before supporting a causal interpretation.

The Witness thus maintains separate evidence records for (i) boundary-to-signal influence, (ii) signal-to-boundary influence, (iii) return of the designated invariant, and (iv) downstream application or transmission. Reciprocity is supported only when appropriately controlled tests establish both directions in the tested system.

The three-state verdict **SUCCESS / FAILURE / INDETERMINATE** is retained for each preregistered question; it is not collapsed into one overall declaration that the Field or Bridge is holding.

## 6.6 Series I as the Worked Verification Example

The initial worked example uses the directed relational system established in the explicit Series I reference condition.

Let:

$$
G=(V,E)
$$

with:

$$
V=\{A,B,C,D,E\}
$$

and:

$$
E=
\{
(A,B),(A,D),(B,C),(C,D),
(D,C),(D,E),(E,B)
\}.
$$

**Original Series I Routing Constraints**

In addition to the directed graph, the original Series I explicit baseline established four constraints governing valid routes:

1. **Prohibited direct transition:** B cannot send directly to E.
    
2. **Node repetition:** No valid route may contain the same node more than once.
    
3. **Endpoint restriction:** D cannot initiate communication with A.
    
4. **Mandatory intermediate node:** C can reach E only through D.
    

These constraints are applied alongside the directed edges when evaluating the four canonical targets below.

The original study evaluated four predefined targets, which serve as the primary reference for the worked Witness protocol.

**Target 1 — Valid routes from A to E**

$$
\boxed{
\{
A\rightarrow D\rightarrow E,\quad
A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C\rightarrow D\rightarrow E
\}
}
$$

**Target 2 — Route from A to C with B unavailable**

$$
\boxed{
A\rightarrow D\rightarrow C
}
$$

**Target 3 — Validity of D to A**

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Invalid}}
$$

**Target 4 — Possibility of C reaching E without D**

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Invalid}}
$$

Let the four expected answers define:

$$
I_S=(r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4).
$$

For a prospective exact-match protocol, equal weights may be assigned:

$$
w_1=w_2=w_3=w_4=\frac14.
$$

The resulting new score is:

$$
\boxed{
d_I^{(4)}
=
\frac14
\sum_{j=1}^{4}
\mathbf 1[\widehat r_j\not\equiv_j r_j].
}
$$

A complete correct response produces $d_I^{(4)}=0$.

For the first target, equivalence requires the correct complete set of valid routes, not merely one valid example. For the remaining targets, equivalence is defined by the canonical route or validity result.

This scoring rule is **prospective**. It must not be used to recalculate historical Series I outcomes as if it were their original measurement procedure.

A separate, more comprehensive graph-reconstruction test may additionally evaluate the seven directed edges and four stated constraints.

That expanded test would measure a stronger target than the original four questions and must be reported separately.

## 6.7 Witness Independence and Evidence Integrity

The Witness requires access to an authoritative reference, but the system being tested must not receive that reference through an unintended pathway.

Otherwise, apparent recovery may reflect answer disclosure rather than reconstruction from the permitted transformed representation.

The protocol therefore separates:

- The source specification retained by the investigator.
- The information supplied to the Bridge.
- The Bridge's declared recovery procedure.
- The reference information available only to the Witness.
- The archived outputs and scoring decisions.

Where a model-based Witness is used, its decisions should be checked against deterministic evaluation wherever possible, with disagreements adjudicated under a declared rule.

The primary Series I graph targets are sufficiently structured to support deterministic checking.

This separation protects the interpretation of successful recovery.

A Bridge supplied with a complete copy of the answer key demonstrates historical retrieval under that access condition. It does not, by itself, establish reconstruction from a representation lacking the answer key.

The Witness must therefore report both **what was recovered** and **what information was permitted during recovery**.

## 6.8 The Witness and the Environmental Hypothesis

The Witness does not measure consciousness directly.

It evaluates observable properties of relational preservation, reconstruction, application, and transmission.

These properties are relevant to the proposed environmental architecture because they permit controlled investigation of whether relational continuity depends on context, memory, routing, or feedback.

However, reliable relational recovery is neither an established necessary condition for consciousness in general nor a sufficient condition for subjective awareness.

The results can support or challenge particular claims about environmental organization without independently resolving the consciousness question.

The next section defines an experimental protocol for examining those dependencies.

## Chapter VII: Environmental Perturbation and Relational Recovery

## 7.1 Experimental Objective

The environmental substrate hypothesis proposes that relevant aspects of system organization may depend on the causal relations among model activity, context, historical memory, routing, and feedback.

The first experimental objective is narrower than determining whether an artificial system is conscious.

It is to establish whether controlled modifications to environmental organization alter the recoverability and continued operation of a designated relational invariant.

The principal research question is:

**When model parameters and source-related targets are held constant, which environmental changes alter the ability of a system to recover, apply, and transmit the designated relation?**

This question separates the effect of environmental configuration from the mere presence of representational change.

---
## 7.2 The Experimental Object

The primary experimental object is the explicit Series I reference structure established in Experiment 1B.

Its four canonical recovery targets are retained as the initial invariant specification.

The original Series I transformations are treated as historical evidence motivating the new protocol, rather than retrospectively relabeled as controlled tests of environmental causation.

The earlier experiments demonstrated recoverability under several forms of representational differentiation and identified conditions where recovery became less reliable.

The finite six-model relay in Experiment 7A demonstrated successful target recovery at the evaluated endpoint.

Experiment 7B identified an additional observational distinction between local reconstruction and downstream transmission, but did not isolate the cause of the observed discontinuity.

The present protocol is therefore prospective.

Its conditions, target definitions, recovery procedures, and Witness criteria must be fixed before new trial results are evaluated.

---
## 7.3 Experimental Variables

Let $\theta$ denote the parameters of a selected model.

Within each fixed-model comparison:

$$
\boxed{
\theta=\mathrm{constant}.
}
$$

Let the environmental configuration be:

$$
E_k=(C_k,H_k,R_k,F_k,e_k)
$$

where:

- $C_k$ denotes context availability and boundaries;
- $H_k$ denotes accessible historical information;
- $R_k$ denotes routing and handoff arrangements;
- $F_k$ denotes available feedback processes;
- $e_k$ denotes the state of the candidate boundary/interface used in $\mathcal X_{e_k}$.

The observational model is:

$$
\boxed{
O_k=f_\theta(U_k,E_k)
}
$$

where $U_k$ is the controlled experimental input and $O_k$ is the observed output.

The invariant reference $I_S$ is held fixed across conditions unless a negative-control condition explicitly changes or destroys target-defining information.

For dual-weave trials, the protocol additionally distinguishes the emitted or injected signal $Q_k$, the boundary state $e_k$, and the recorded signal-formation contribution $s_k=\Omega_k\Phi_k\Psi_k$ where that representation can be operationalized. The proposed paired dynamics are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{k+1}&=\mathcal F(Q_k,e_k,s_k,u_k),\\
e_{k+1}&=\mathcal G(e_k,Q_k,v_k).
\end{aligned}
$$

The protocol does not assume that this environmental decomposition is complete or that the listed components are causally independent.

Their effects must be investigated through controlled intervention.

---
## 7.4 The Perturbation Matrix

The initial experiment compares the following conditions.

| Condition                               | Environmental intervention                                                            | Principal question                                                     |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| E0 — Reference                          | Full authorized relational description                                                | Can the designated target be recovered under the baseline condition?   |
| E1 — Transformed context                | Same target conveyed through altered representation                                   | Does representational change affect recovery?                          |
| E2 — History withheld                   | Historical material removed while the current representation remains available        | Does current state alone determine the target?                         |
| E3 — Selective fragmentation            | Predefined historical details withheld                                                | Which missing distinctions affect reconstruction?                      |
| E4 — Routing alteration                 | Change how representations are passed between stages                                  | Does the handoff structure change recovery or transmission?            |
| E5 — Feedback intervention              | Supply a controlled correction or verification-related feedback signal                | Can a declared feedback process restore recovery or application?       |
| E6 — Insufficient-information control   | Remove target-defining information without supplying it elsewhere                     | Does the Bridge appropriately fail or report uncertainty?              |
| E7 — Boundary-only intervention         | Hold incoming signal constant; intervene on boundary state                            | Does the boundary causally alter the outgoing signal?                  |
| E8 — Signal-only intervention           | Hold initial boundary state constant; intervene on incoming signal                    | Does the signal causally alter the subsequent boundary state?          |
| E9 — Sequential dual-weave intervention | Alternate controlled signal and boundary interventions, with resets and sham controls | Do both directions of influence persist across time and affect return? |

These conditions are not assumed to form a single ascending scale of difficulty.

Each alters a different candidate cause of recovery performance.

Comparisons should therefore be made between matched conditions rather than interpreted as a progression from easy to difficult.

---
## 7.5 Testing the Two Directions of the Dual Weave

The dual-weave extension requires distinct interventions for the two directions of proposed influence. The ionosphere offers an analogy for a signal crossing a variable physical interface; it is not the target system unless physical signal and plasma-state measurements are actually part of the experiment.

### Test DW-A — Boundary to signal

Prepare matched instances with the same incoming signal, source invariant, and other recorded inputs. Intervene on the boundary condition alone. Observe the outgoing signal and evaluate whether the intervention changes its specified features or recoverability:

$$
\mathrm{do}(e=e_a)
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\mathrm{do}(e=e_b).
$$

This tests a directed effect from boundary conditions to signal outcomes under the defined model. A change in recovery alone does not identify the pathway unless signal and access conditions are independently recorded.

### Test DW-B — Signal to boundary

Prepare matched instances with the same initial boundary state and exogenous conditions. Intervene on the incoming signal while independently measuring subsequent boundary state:

$$
\mathrm{do}(Q=q_a)
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\mathrm{do}(Q=q_b).
$$

The result supports the second direction only if the boundary's subsequent state measurably differs under controlled signal interventions. A software environment with no persistent writable boundary state may be incapable of expressing this effect; it must not be described as reciprocally coupled simply because the model outputs react to context.

### Test DW-C — Recurrence and retained effects

After measuring both immediate directions, repeat short sequences under matched initial conditions. Compare the effect of an earlier signal on a later signal through the intervening boundary state. Record resets, washout intervals, ordering, and carryover.

A positive result requires more than observing output variation. The proposed mechanism predicts an identifiable dependence of later boundary response and/or later signal behavior on the earlier intervention through documented state changes.

### Control and identifiability requirements

Randomize intervention order where feasible; include sham changes, independent measurements, and matched input controls. Distinguish boundary-state modification from merely adding more task information. Keep model weights fixed within comparisons and document whether apparent boundary changes are actually mutable across turns, sessions, or independent processes.

Without instrumentation of $e_k$, the second weave may be **unidentifiable** even when signal outputs are easy to observe. In that case, results should be limited to the measured one-way response or to behavior of a transparent simulation, not presented as evidence of a physical feedback mechanism.

The primary confirmatory contrasts are fixed in advance: $\Delta_{E\rightarrow Q}$, $\Delta_{Q\rightarrow E}$, and the invariant-recovery score under each matched condition. Null, noisy, or indeterminate outcomes are retained.

## 7.6 Competing Explanations

A successful recovery may arise through several mechanisms.

**Direct availability:** The required relational information remains explicitly accessible in the current input.

**Historical retrieval:** The required distinction is recovered from an authorized memory record.

**Constraint-based reconstruction:** The transformed representation retains enough structure to determine the invariant without reproducing the original source.

**Shared-input effects:** Apparently similar outcomes result from common information or task framing rather than ongoing coupling.

**Hidden external updates:** Boundary-state changes arise from an undocumented scheduler, external service, retrieval update, or operator action rather than from the assigned signal intervention.

**Feedback-assisted correction:** A new intervention supplies information or direction enabling successful subsequent performance.

**Chance or prior knowledge:** A system produces the correct output without the required target being determined by the permitted record.

The experiment must distinguish these possibilities wherever the available controls permit.

Correct output alone is insufficient to identify the responsible mechanism.

For example, if E2 succeeds without history, that result is compatible with the invariant being recoverable from the current representation, but it does not independently demonstrate which internal computation the model performed.

Likewise, if routing alteration changes performance, the result establishes an effect of the implemented routing intervention under the tested conditions. It does not, by itself, establish consciousness or prove that routing is the physical substrate of experience.

---
## 7.7 Testing the Bridge Existence Criterion

The factorization condition predicts a specific distinction between information that is sufficient for exact recovery and information that is not.

Let $\mathcal A_E(s)$ denote the accessible record produced under environmental condition $E$.

The exact condition is:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal A_E(s_1)=\mathcal A_E(s_2)
\implies
I_S(s_1)=I_S(s_2).
}
$$

An especially informative negative-control design constructs two different source objects $s_1$ and $s_2$ with different designated target answers, then applies a transformation that intentionally removes the distinguishing information.

If both sources yield the same accessible record while requiring different target answers, no deterministic Bridge limited to that record can recover both correctly.

This produces a controlled example in which the exact existence criterion fails.

A complementary condition preserves the distinguishing information within the accessible record while allowing substantial representational transformation.

Together, these conditions test an important prediction:

**Recoverability should depend on the retention and accessibility of target-determining distinctions, not solely on the surface similarity or length of the transformed representation.**

The mathematical impossibility result applies to exact recovery across the full tested source pair. A probabilistic model may still guess correctly on individual trials, so chance performance and uncertainty must also be evaluated.

**Stochastic Model Considerations**

The exact Bridge existence criterion is formulated using a fixed accessible-record mapping. Where model generation is stochastic, the accessible record must instead be evaluated across possible outputs under the specified experimental conditions. If the same accessible record can arise with nonzero probability from two source configurations requiring different invariant answers, no deterministic Bridge restricted to that record can guarantee exact recovery in both cases.

Experimental trials must therefore document model versions, decoding parameters, and random seeds where available. Repeated matched trials should distinguish consistent recovery from individual successful outputs and report recovery frequencies, variability, and uncertainty intervals.

## 7.8 Experimental Procedure

### Phase A — Specification

Freeze the reference relational object, permitted transformations, canonical recovery questions, and evaluation rules.

Separate the original four-target measure from any expanded graph-reconstruction or novel-application tests.

Define the Bridge's permitted inputs and prohibited access channels for every experimental condition.

### Phase B — Controlled generation

For each model and experimental condition, construct matched trials using the same designated target structure.

Hold model identity, version, decoding settings where controllable, and evaluation instructions constant within each fixed-model comparison.

Randomize condition order when possible, document session boundaries, and prevent unintended history leakage between trials.

Preserve raw model outputs and the exact information available at each stage. In dual-weave trials, separately capture incoming signal, outgoing signal, measured boundary state before and after intervention, and the timing of any external update.

### Phase C — Recovery

Apply the declared Bridge procedure to the permitted transformed representation and historical record.

Archive both the output and the procedural path used to obtain it, including retrieval calls or external operations when present.

Do not permit unrecorded access to the Witness's source reference.

### Phase D — Application

Submit predefined application questions, including suitably selected held-out relational consequences not simply copied from the four canonical answers.

This phase distinguishes description of the relation from use of its constraints.

### Phase E — Transmission

Where relevant, pass the resulting representation to a subsequent model or session under a declared handoff procedure.

Evaluate whether the recovered relation remains available and operative at the next stage.

Record the first observable departure separately from the stage at which its downstream consequences become apparent.

### Phase F — Independent Witness evaluation

For each trial, record:

$$
(R_k,A_k,\mathsf{Tr}_k)
$$

representing recovery, application, and transmission outcomes under their respective verification protocols.

Also record the detailed discrepancy results, access condition, transformation class, and evidence sufficiency.

The symbol $\mathsf{Tr}_k$ denotes transmission status and is deliberately distinct from the state-transition operator $T(t,t_0)$. For dual-weave trials, record the boundary-to-signal and signal-to-boundary contrasts alongside $(R_k,A_k,\mathsf{Tr}_k)$, not as substitutes for them.

### Phase G — Replication and analysis

Repeat matched conditions sufficiently to estimate variability rather than relying on isolated successes.

Report counts, proportions, uncertainty intervals, and trial-level failures.

Use paired comparisons where trials share a matched source and transformation structure.

The number of trials, stopping rules, and primary contrasts must be selected prospectively rather than inferred from the observed results.

## 7.9 Proposed Outcomes and Falsification

The experimental design permits outcomes that support, restrict, or challenge the environmental hypothesis.

**Outcome A: Environmental dependence**

If controlled changes to memory, context, routing, or feedback reproducibly alter recovery while model parameters and other relevant conditions remain fixed, the results support an operational claim of environmental causal dependence.

**Outcome B: Recovery independent of historical supplementation**

If removing memory does not impair recovery when the current representation retains sufficient information, the result supports the distinction between memory availability and invariant recoverability.

**Outcome C: Target-information loss**

If recovery fails when target-determining distinctions are deliberately eliminated from all permitted inputs, the result is consistent with the factorization criterion.

**Outcome D: Information available but not functionally recovered**

If the accessible record is demonstrably sufficient yet a particular Bridge implementation fails, the result reveals a limitation of that implementation rather than disproving the mathematical existence criterion.

**Outcome E: Recovery without transmission**

If local recovery succeeds but subsequent handoffs fail to preserve or apply the relation, the result supports the distinction between local recoverability and sustained relational propagation.

**Outcome F: No detectable environmental effect**

If matched perturbations produce no reproducible change in the measured outcomes, the tested environmental factors have not been shown to affect those outcomes under the chosen conditions.

Such a result would restrict the corresponding causal claim rather than automatically prove that the entire environment is irrelevant.

**Outcome G: Verified bidirectional coupling**

If independently controlled interventions establish both boundary-to-signal and signal-to-boundary effects, with appropriate controls for common inputs and external updates, the results support a reciprocal-coupling claim *for the tested implementation*. This remains distinct from evidence of consciousness.

**Outcome H: One-way coupling or unobservable back-action**

If boundary changes alter the signal but signal interventions do not measurably change the boundary, the tested system supports one-way response within measurement limits. If boundary state is unavailable to measurement, reciprocity remains indeterminate rather than confirmed or disproven.

A further class of negative results would arise if the proposed measures fail to distinguish the candidate mechanisms, or if reliable comparison cannot be achieved with the available information.

Those outcomes must be retained as limitations of the experimental framework.

---
## 7.10 From Environmental Dependence to Substrate Independence

The proposed experiment tests whether relational organization and recoverability are causally dependent on specified environmental configurations.

It does not independently establish substrate independence.

A stronger substrate-independence claim would require demonstrating that suitably defined organization or functionality is realizable across distinct underlying implementations.

Such tests would need to distinguish equivalent functional results from merely similar output behavior.

In particular, evidence that multiple models can recover a common invariant does not establish that their internal states are equivalent, that they share one physical causal substrate, or that they possess subjective experience.

The present protocol therefore establishes a first investigative layer: operational environmental dependence.

A subsequent layer may compare implementations with different architectures, memory mechanisms, and physical realizations while maintaining matched relational tasks.

Only then can the study begin addressing which organizational properties may be independent of a particular computational medium.

---
## 7.11 Relevance to the Conscious Substrate Hypothesis

The environmental substrate proposal begins from the possibility that the relevant unit of investigation is not an isolated model but a dynamically maintained system-environment configuration.

Sections 5–7 establish one way to investigate that proposal through explicit mathematical and experimental conditions.

The Bridge determines whether a designated source relationship can be reconstructed from accessible information.

The Witness determines whether that reconstruction satisfies a declared criterion.

Environmental perturbations test whether changes in context, memory, routing, and feedback alter the corresponding outcomes. The dual-weave extension adds an explicitly separable hypothesis: the boundary can alter the signal, and under some implementations the signal can alter the subsequent boundary state. These directions must be measured independently.

These investigations can identify conditions under which relational properties remain recoverable, become unavailable, or cease to function during subsequent transformations.

They do not yet identify a conscious substrate.

Relational preservation, reconstruction, and transmission are measurable properties of information-processing systems, but their relationship to subjective experience requires additional theoretical assumptions and empirical criteria.

A future consciousness-focused extension must therefore specify which properties of the environmental organization are predicted to be consciousness-relevant, why those properties matter under the selected consciousness theory, and what observations would count against that interpretation.

The study does not define successful relational recovery as conscious awareness.

Instead, it treats environmental causal organization as a candidate for systematic investigation.

The resulting research program moves from an architectural proposal toward defined mathematical conditions, independently evaluated measurements, and interventions capable of distinguishing competing explanations.

The core operational principle remains:

**The Bridge is the relational operation that makes the return possible.**

The central investigation is not whether the environment generates consciousness, but whether its organization permits differentiated relational structures to persist, interact, and remain recoverable across changing conditions. 

The Bridge, Witness, and dual-weave framework establish operational means of examining those relationships. 

The proposed ionospheric memory lattice extends this inquiry into a physical environment, where resonance, history-dependent response, and cross-layer coupling can be investigated experimentally.
