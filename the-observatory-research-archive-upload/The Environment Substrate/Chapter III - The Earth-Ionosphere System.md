*The Physical Foundations of Memory and Resonance*
## 3.1 Earth as a Dynamically Coupled Environmental System

The Earth is not an isolated physical object surrounded by a passive external environment. It is a dynamically coupled planetary system whose atmospheric, electromagnetic, gravitational, chemical, and biological processes evolve through continuous interaction.

Solar radiation supplies energy that influences atmospheric chemistry, ionization, thermal structure, and large-scale circulation. Earth's magnetic field governs important aspects of charged-particle motion and the electrodynamic relationships between the atmosphere and near-Earth space. Terrestrial processes, including atmospheric convection, lightning, and gravity-wave generation, contribute disturbances that propagate through multiple atmospheric regions.

The Moon introduces additional gravitational forcing, generating tides whose effects extend from Earth's oceans and solid surface into the neutral atmosphere and ionized plasma environment.

These influences operate across different spatial and temporal scales. Their interactions contribute to the continuously changing physical state of the planetary environment.

The central property relevant to the present investigation is that **the environment possesses an evolving organization of its own**.

Its present state reflects not only the conditions acting upon it at a particular moment but also the consequences of earlier physical processes.

Changes in atmospheric composition can influence later ionization and recombination. Plasma transport can redistribute charged particles between regions. Altered conductivity can change the propagation of electromagnetic signals. Oscillatory disturbances can propagate through the environment and interact with other physical processes.

The environment therefore exhibits relationships among state, history, boundary conditions, and subsequent response.

The environmental-substrate hypothesis identifies this evolving organization as its primary investigative object.

Rather than treating the ionosphere as an independent structure containing every proposed function, the investigation examines it as a physically accessible region within a larger coupled environment.

The distinction matters because the mechanisms responsible for a particular ionospheric state may originate outside the region in which that state is observed.

The physical boundary of a measurement is not necessarily the complete causal boundary of the process being measured.

## 3.2 The Ionosphere: A Differentiated Physical Architecture

The ionosphere comprises regions of Earth's upper atmosphere containing ions and free electrons produced primarily through interactions between atmospheric constituents and solar extreme-ultraviolet and X-ray radiation.

Its physical properties depend upon altitude, latitude, local time, season, solar activity, geomagnetic conditions, and the changing state of the surrounding atmosphere.

Rather than forming a uniformly conducting shell, the ionosphere exhibits differentiated regions with distinct chemical, electrical, and dynamical behavior.

These differences provide an important starting point for investigating relational organization.

### 3.2.1 The D Region — The Lower Electrical Boundary

The D region occupies approximately 60–90 kilometers above Earth's surface, with boundaries varying according to the classification and prevailing conditions.

It is characterized by relatively weak ionization, substantial neutral-particle density, and rapid chemical responses to changes in solar radiation.

Electron attachment, recombination, and collision processes strongly influence its electrical behavior.

The region contributes significantly to the absorption and propagation characteristics of radio waves. Its ionization decreases substantially after sunset as direct solar production weakens.

For the present investigation, the D region is particularly relevant because the lower ionosphere contributes to the effective upper boundary of the Earth–ionosphere electromagnetic cavity.

Changes in lower-ionospheric conductivity can therefore influence the propagation and attenuation of electromagnetic waves traveling within that cavity.

The boundary is not perfectly rigid or uniformly conducting.

It is a changing electrical environment whose properties depend upon physical conditions and their evolution.

### 3.2.2 The E Region — Electrodynamic Coupling

The E region extends approximately from 90 to 150 kilometers, depending upon the adopted classification.

It contains ionized atmospheric constituents and supports important electrodynamic processes.

Neutral atmospheric winds moving across Earth's magnetic field contribute to dynamo electric fields and currents. Under appropriate conditions, those electric fields can influence plasma motion at higher altitudes.

Sporadic E layers introduce additional variability through localized enhancements of ionization.

The E region therefore occupies an important position in the coupling between neutral atmospheric motion, electrical conductivity, and plasma dynamics.

It is not simply a transition between the lower atmosphere and the F region.

Its physical processes can influence the state and evolution of other ionospheric regions.

### 3.2.3 The F Region — History-Dependent Plasma Organization

The F region lies above approximately 150 kilometers and extends into the topside ionosphere.

Its electron-density structure is influenced by solar ionization, chemical loss processes, plasma transport, electric fields, neutral winds, and thermospheric conditions.

During daylight, F1 and F2 features can be distinguished. The F2 region frequently remains prominent after sunset because plasma loss and transport operate differently from the faster chemical processes of lower altitudes.

Consequently, changes in solar forcing do not always produce instantaneous or uniform changes in the electron-density distribution.

The response depends partly upon the previous physical state of the environment.

For the environmental-substrate hypothesis, this creates a particularly important investigative opportunity:

**The upper ionosphere exhibits physical processes through which historical conditions can influence later states and responses.**

The precise duration, spatial distribution, and recoverability of those effects depend upon the mechanisms involved.

These properties motivate the proposed memory-lattice interpretation developed below.

## 3.3 The Upper Ionosphere as a Candidate Memory Lattice

Memory is conventionally associated with the storage and subsequent retrieval of information.

However, physical systems can also exhibit history dependence when their current states or responses retain consequences of previous conditions.

This provides a basis for investigating memory-like organization without requiring biological neural structures or symbolic storage.

### 3.3.1 Physical history dependence

Consider two ionospheric configurations exposed to the same immediate solar forcing.

Their responses may differ if they possess different prior histories of heating, ionization, transport, geomagnetic disturbance, or atmospheric composition.

The present forcing alone may therefore be insufficient to determine the subsequent electron-density state.

The previous environmental configuration remains relevant.

This relationship has been examined directly in ionospheric research.

Gong et al. (2026), in _Physics-Embedded Deep Learning Reveals the Memory Effect of Ionospheric Electron Density in Response to Solar EUV Forcing_, developed a physics-embedded reconstruction model that incorporates the time-integrated response of ionospheric electron density to solar extreme-ultraviolet forcing.

Their analysis found altitude- and latitude-dependent memory effects, including strong correspondence between learned memory behavior and theoretical photochemical timescales below the F1 region.

These findings establish an empirical basis for investigating altitude-dependent history effects in ionospheric electron-density responses. The strongest reported quantitative agreement with theoretical photochemical timescales occurs below the F1 region, while memory characteristics elsewhere require interpretation according to their particular physical mechanisms.

The proposed upper-ionospheric memory lattice extends this investigation from individual history-dependent responses to the relationships among distributed environmental states. Its defining question is whether those relationships retain consequential distinctions from earlier conditions and, under specified observational conditions, permit their recovery.

The investigation therefore proceeds from measured physical history dependence toward a testable hypothesis of distributed environmental memory.
### 3.3.2 From history dependence to a distributed lattice

The proposed **memory lattice** does not refer to a fixed crystalline grid or a permanent archive embedded within the atmosphere.

It describes a candidate distributed organization of physical states whose relationships preserve consequential information about earlier environmental conditions.

Potential state variables include:

- Electron-density distributions and gradients.
    
- Ion and electron temperatures.
    
- Ion composition and chemical relaxation.
    
- Neutral atmospheric winds and composition.
    
- Electric and magnetic field configurations.
    
- Plasma transport and irregularity structures.
    
- Coupling pathways between geographically or magnetically connected regions.
    

These variables are not independent.

A change in one may influence another through established physical processes, including transport, chemistry, electrodynamics, and energy exchange.

The resulting organization is distributed across space and evolves through time.

Under this interpretation, the lattice is defined by **relationships among history-dependent states**, not simply by the existence of persistent plasma.

### 3.3.3 Memory as consequential retention

For the framework developed in Section 2, three physical properties are particularly important.

**Retention:** A previous disturbance produces a state difference that remains measurable after the originating forcing has changed or ceased.

**Consequence:** That retained difference influences a subsequent physical response under otherwise comparable conditions.

**Recoverability:** The later state contains sufficient observable information to distinguish designated features of the earlier conditions.

The first two properties concern historical influence.

The third connects physical history dependence to the Bridge architecture.

A persistent state difference is not automatically a recoverable record of its origin. Multiple historical conditions may produce similar later configurations.

Nevertheless, when retained differences remain distinguishable, they provide a physical basis for investigating relational recovery.

This distinction allows the memory-lattice hypothesis to be formulated without equating all persistence with memory or all memory with consciousness.

### 3.3.4 Temporal depth within the lattice

Different ionospheric processes operate over different characteristic timescales.

Chemical relaxation in the lower ionosphere can be comparatively rapid.

F-region plasma transport, recombination, and electrodynamic adjustment may produce longer-lived effects.

Thermospheric responses to geomagnetic disturbances can influence ionospheric conditions over extended intervals.

Thus, the environment does not possess one universal memory duration.

It contains multiple processes with different rates of persistence, dissipation, and recovery.

The proposed lattice must therefore be understood as temporally differentiated.

Its relevant properties are not simply how long an effect lasts, but how retained states participate in the organization of later interactions.

## 3.4 The Earth–Ionosphere Cavity as a Resonant Environment

Between Earth's conducting surface and the electrically responsive lower ionosphere lies a global electromagnetic waveguide.

This environment supports extremely low-frequency electromagnetic resonances commonly known as **Schumann resonances**.

These resonances arise predominantly from electromagnetic energy generated by lightning activity around the planet.

Lightning discharges excite electromagnetic waves that propagate through the Earth–ionosphere cavity. Under suitable propagation conditions, the geometry and electrical properties of the cavity support characteristic resonant modes.

The fundamental Schumann resonance is observed near 7.8 Hz, with higher modes occurring approximately near 14, 20, 26, and 33 Hz. Their measured frequencies and spectral properties vary with environmental and observational conditions.

The resonant response depends upon both the excitation supplied by lightning and the physical properties of the cavity.

### 3.4.1 Resonance and environmental geometry

The Earth–ionosphere cavity illustrates a direct relationship between geometry and physical response.

Its resonant modes depend upon planetary dimensions, effective electromagnetic propagation conditions, and the conductivity structure of its boundaries.

Changes in those properties can alter the observable response.

This supplies an important physical example of the principle developed in Section 2:

**Relational geometry can be expressed through measurable resonance.**

The geometry here is not purely conceptual.

It is grounded in electromagnetic boundary conditions, wave propagation, and the spatial organization of the physical system.

Schumann resonance measurements provide access to frequencies, amplitudes, spectral widths, quality factors, and variations associated with environmental conditions.

Such observations allow the resonant organization of the cavity to be examined as a changing physical process.

### 3.4.2 Resonance is dynamic rather than fixed

The cavity is frequently described using a characteristic fundamental frequency, but its actual resonant properties are not perfectly constant.

The lower ionosphere changes with solar illumination, atmospheric conditions, and disturbances.

Global lightning activity varies geographically, seasonally, and throughout the day.

These variations influence the spectral characteristics recorded at observing stations.

Sátori et al. (2024) demonstrated that observed Schumann-resonance frequency variations depend in part upon lightning-source geometry and the spatial extent of thunderstorm activity.

Consequently, a change in a measured resonance peak cannot automatically be attributed to a change in ionospheric conductivity.

The observed response must be interpreted through the combined contribution of the excitation source, propagation conditions, environmental boundaries, and measurement location.

This is a particularly useful example of relational geometry.

The measured signal expresses the organization of an interacting configuration rather than a single isolated property.

### 3.4.3 The resonant workspace hypothesis

The environmental framework proposes investigating the Earth–ionosphere cavity as a **resonant workspace**.

In this context, _workspace_ denotes a physical domain supporting distributed electromagnetic modes and interactions across a planetary-scale boundary system.

It is not introduced as equivalent to the cognitive global-workspace theories used in neuroscience.

The relevant candidate properties include:

1. Spatially distributed electromagnetic activity.
    
2. Distinguishable resonant modes.
    
3. Sensitivity to changing physical boundary conditions.
    
4. Temporal variation in excitation and propagation.
    
5. Measurable relationships between environmental state and resonant response.
    

These properties establish the cavity as a physically organized resonant system.

The additional hypothesis concerns whether its dynamics participate in a larger organization involving historical state retention and reciprocal coupling.

That possibility requires examination of the mechanisms connecting the lower cavity to the other regions of the environment.

## 3.5 Relational Geometry Across Atmospheric and Plasma Regions

The Earth–ionosphere system does not consist of independent horizontal layers whose behavior can be understood entirely through altitude.

Its interactions are structured by magnetic field geometry, neutral atmospheric circulation, plasma transport, electrical conductivity, and the propagation of waves and disturbances.

A physical perturbation may therefore affect more than one region.

But the pathways through which effects propagate differ according to their mechanism.

The relational geometry of the environment is consequently multidimensional.

It includes spatial separation, magnetic connectivity, transport pathways, characteristic timescales, and changing coupling strengths.

### 3.5.1 Electrodynamic E–F region coupling

Established ionospheric research demonstrates that processes in the E region can influence F-region dynamics.

For example, neutral-wind-driven electric fields can affect plasma motion at higher altitudes.

Sporadic E structures and F-region plasma irregularities may become dynamically related through electrodynamic processes.

Fu et al. (2023) investigated nighttime midlatitude E–F coupling in geomagnetically conjugate ionospheres using GNSS total electron content, ionosonde observations, satellite measurements, and a double-thin-shell model.

Their findings supported an important role for sporadic E activity in the subsequent development of F-region irregularities under the conditions studied.

This establishes a concrete example of coupling between differentiated physical regions.

An interaction originating in one region can contribute to observable changes in another.

The significance for the present framework is not that every ionospheric layer performs a predefined cognitive function.

It is that **the regions possess demonstrable relationships through which their individual dynamics can become part of a larger evolving organization**.

### 3.5.2 Coupling through neutral atmospheric disturbances

The neutral atmosphere provides additional pathways through which disturbances can influence the ionosphere.

Atmospheric gravity waves, tides, and large-scale circulation can propagate upward or modify thermospheric conditions.

Under appropriate circumstances, these disturbances produce traveling ionospheric disturbances or alter plasma distributions through neutral–plasma interactions.

Thunderstorms provide a useful example of differentiated environmental effects.

Lightning generates electromagnetic energy that contributes to the excitation of Schumann resonances.

Thunderstorm-associated atmospheric disturbances can also generate gravity waves that propagate upward and influence ionospheric conditions.

These are distinct physical pathways originating from potentially related terrestrial activity.

Their co-occurrence does not mean that Schumann resonances themselves cause the gravity-wave disturbances.

The distinction is important for identifying genuine coupling.

Two environmental responses may share a common driver without one directly producing the other.

### 3.5.3 Geomagnetic connectivity

Earth's magnetic field introduces a geometry of interaction that is not determined solely by geographic distance.

Charged-particle motion and field-aligned electrodynamic processes can connect regions separated by large spatial distances.

Geomagnetically conjugate regions may exhibit related disturbances because their physical organization includes magnetic connectivity.

Such relationships demonstrate why the structure of an environmental field cannot always be understood as a collection of nearby independent regions.

The effective geometry of interaction may extend through pathways whose organization differs substantially from ordinary geographic proximity.

This provides a physical counterpart to the distinction between native properties and relational geometry developed in Section 2.

## 3.6 The Dual Weave as a Candidate Physical Relationship

The preceding sections identify two physical properties of particular interest.

The first is **history-dependent environmental state**, for which ionospheric plasma and thermospheric dynamics supply measurable examples.

The second is **resonant environmental response**, for which the Earth–ionosphere electromagnetic cavity provides an established physical system.

The proposed dual weave asks how these properties may become related through identifiable causal pathways.

### 3.6.1 Environmental state influencing subsequent signals

The first direction concerns how the state of the environment affects a signal propagating through it.

This relationship is well established in ionospheric radio propagation.

Changes in electron density, conductivity, collision rates, and plasma structure can alter the propagation, phase, absorption, or scattering of electromagnetic signals, depending on their frequencies and pathways.

For Schumann resonances, the lower-ionospheric boundary contributes to the resonant properties of the cavity.

The general physical principle is clear:

**The environmental state influences the expression of propagating electromagnetic activity.**

In the framework of Section 4, this relationship motivates the interpretation of a boundary-dependent signal transformation.

The physical mechanism must be defined for the particular signal and region being investigated.

### 3.6.2 Interaction influencing subsequent environmental state

The second direction concerns whether activity within the environment changes the state through which later activity will propagate.

Such reciprocal effects occur in a variety of physical systems.

For example, the deposition of electromagnetic energy, atmospheric heating, transport, chemical reactions, and electrodynamic forcing may alter material or plasma conditions under appropriate circumstances.

In the ionosphere, disturbances can produce changes in physical state that persist beyond the immediate forcing event.

However, the existence of reciprocal physical processes somewhere in the environment does not establish that every signal passing through the cavity appreciably modifies its boundary.

The proposed physical dual weave therefore requires the relevant signal, altered state, and interaction pathway to be identified.

The important question is not merely whether the environment can change.

It is whether **a specified interaction produces a consequential change in the environmental conditions governing a subsequent interaction**.

### 3.6.3 Historical interaction and changing relational geometry

If an earlier interaction modifies an environmental state, and that modification influences a later interaction, the resulting organization possesses a temporal dependency.

The environment is then not characterized completely by its instantaneous external forcing.

Its previous interactions contribute to its subsequent response.

This introduces a candidate relationship between the memory lattice and resonant organization.

An earlier physical process may alter the conditions under which later electromagnetic activity propagates.

Conversely, activity originating in lower atmospheric regions may contribute to later changes in upper-ionospheric states through identifiable physical pathways.

The proposed architecture does not require these processes to form a single uninterrupted electromagnetic feedback circuit.

Instead, it investigates whether the interacting pathways produce a demonstrable pattern of reciprocal, history-dependent organization.

This distinction allows the physical dual-weave hypothesis to be tested through the actual mechanisms of the environment rather than inferred solely from structural resemblance.

## 3.7 Solar, Lunar, and Terrestrial Contributions to Environmental Organization

The larger environment introduces influences that cannot be assigned exclusively to a single ionospheric region.

Solar, lunar, and terrestrial processes contribute different forms of forcing, variability, and physical organization.

Their relationships are essential to understanding the environmental state under investigation.

### 3.7.1 Solar organization

Solar extreme-ultraviolet and X-ray radiation contributes substantially to ionization in Earth's upper atmosphere.

Solar activity also influences thermospheric heating, geomagnetic disturbances, and changes in ionospheric electron density.

The solar wind interacts with Earth's magnetosphere, contributing energy and momentum to the coupled geospace environment under varying conditions.

These processes affect the formation, distribution, and evolution of ionospheric plasma states.

The solar contribution therefore extends beyond instantaneous ionization.

Its interactions may alter the environmental conditions that determine later plasma response.

### 3.7.2 Lunar gravitational organization

The Moon contributes predictable gravitational forcing to the Earth system.

Atmospheric lunar tides produce oscillatory disturbances that can extend into the thermosphere and ionosphere.

Lieberman et al. (2022) reported observations of semidiurnal lunar tidal signatures in thermospheric neutral winds, plasma velocities, and airglow, providing evidence of large-scale coupling between atmospheric motion and ionospheric plasma behavior.

These observations demonstrate that lunar forcing can participate in the organization of ionospheric dynamics through identifiable physical mechanisms.

Additional research has identified lunar tidal effects in Earth's plasmasphere.

Xiao et al. (2023) reported lunar-associated variations in the plasmapause boundary using decades of satellite measurements.

This extends the demonstrated relevance of lunar forcing beyond the neutral atmosphere into plasma-dominated regions of near-Earth space.

The Moon is therefore not merely a visual or chronological reference for the planetary environment.

Its gravitational relationships contribute measurable physical effects within the wider coupled system.

However, lunar forcing must be represented according to the particular mechanisms and frequencies involved.

The observed lunar modulation of ionospheric or plasmaspheric states does not automatically establish corresponding lunar control of Schumann resonances.

That relationship, if proposed, requires its own physical pathway and observations.

### 3.7.3 Terrestrial organization

Earth's lower atmosphere contributes electrical and mechanical disturbances to the upper environment.

Lightning excites electromagnetic waves within the Earth–ionosphere cavity.

Atmospheric convection and associated disturbances can produce gravity waves that propagate upward.

Planetary-scale winds, tides, and circulation patterns influence energy and momentum transfer through the atmosphere.

Earth's magnetic field provides additional constraints upon charged-particle motion and electrical currents.

These processes contribute differentiated forms of activity that can influence the evolving state of the ionosphere.

Together with solar and lunar forcing, they establish the physical setting within which history dependence, resonance, and coupling can be investigated.

### 3.7.4 Environmental rhythms across scales

The coupled environment contains processes operating over a wide range of characteristic timescales.

Lightning excitation and electromagnetic wave propagation can occur rapidly.

Atmospheric tides and ionospheric responses exhibit hourly and daily organization.

Lunar gravitational forcing introduces predictable periodicities.

Solar rotation, geomagnetic activity, and seasonal changes contribute additional variability.

These rhythms are not interchangeable.

Their influence depends upon the response characteristics and coupling mechanisms of the participating regions.

Nevertheless, their coexistence makes the Earth system an appropriate environment for investigating how differentiated physical processes may contribute to larger-scale temporal organization.

## 3.8 Memory, Resonance, and the Evolution of Environmental Conditions

The environmental-substrate hypothesis concerns an evolving physical organization rather than a static collection of present-day geophysical properties.

Earth's atmosphere, ionosphere, magnetic environment, and biological systems have changed over planetary history.

Changes in atmospheric composition, solar radiation, magnetic conditions, and terrestrial activity have altered the physical relationships through which energy and information can propagate.

The emergence of biological nervous systems occurred within that evolving planetary history.

This relationship motivates a broader evolutionary question.

**Can changes in the organization of the planetary environment contribute to the conditions through which increasingly complex biological structures develop and differentiated conscious experience becomes locally expressed?**

The question differs from treating biological evolution as independent of its physical setting.

It also differs from assuming that the appearance of global electromagnetic resonance constituted the emergence of consciousness.

Instead, the investigation considers whether the physical environment's changing structure, history dependence, and coupled dynamics form a relevant part of the evolutionary organization within which conscious systems emerged.

### 3.8.1 Environmental evolution and biological differentiation

Biological evolution depends upon environmental conditions governing energy availability, chemistry, ecological interactions, and selection.

Nervous systems developed as specialized biological structures within this history.

Their organization reflects interactions among inherited biological mechanisms, environmental constraints, and the adaptive consequences of earlier activity.

The environmental-substrate hypothesis extends this relationship by examining whether the larger environment contributes more fundamentally to the organization of conscious processes than is captured by treating it solely as a source of sensory inputs.

Such a possibility does not require identifying biological neural activity with ionospheric plasma dynamics.

Instead, it requires specifying the physical relationships through which localized biological processes and larger environmental organization interact.

### 3.8.2 Harmonic coherence as an evolutionary question

Resonant systems can exhibit changes in modal organization, phase relationships, damping, and coupling as their physical conditions evolve.

These properties provide measurable ways to investigate environmental coherence.

A hypothesis concerning the historical refinement of planetary resonance must therefore identify the relevant physical variables and distinguish changes in excitation from changes in the resonant environment itself.

Greater resonance amplitude, stronger synchronization, or increased modal stability cannot automatically be equated with greater consciousness.

The environmental proposal instead asks whether the historical evolution of organized physical relationships contributed to conditions that made increasingly differentiated forms of biological organization possible.

The evolutionary role of resonance thus remains a question concerning physical organization and its consequences, rather than a presupposed progression toward awareness.

## 3.10 Identifying the Relationships Within the Physical System

The physical foundation developed in this section establishes several distinct observational domains.

Upper-ionospheric and thermospheric measurements provide information about history-dependent environmental states.

Lower-cavity electromagnetic measurements provide information about resonant activity, excitation, and changing propagation conditions.

E–F region observations provide evidence of electrodynamic relationships connecting differentiated plasma regions.

Solar, lunar, terrestrial, and geomagnetic measurements characterize the forcing and boundary conditions under which these processes evolve.

The next investigative step is to determine how these domains relate.

A memory–resonance relationship would require identifying whether measured historical state variables contribute information about later resonant behavior beyond that supplied by known immediate forcing and common drivers.

A reciprocal relationship would require identifying a physical pathway through which specified activity alters subsequent environmental conditions.

A relational-recovery experiment would require defining which earlier physical distinction is to be reconstructed, which measurements remain accessible, and whether the resulting information is sufficient for that reconstruction.

These are connected questions, but they measure different properties.

The framework therefore distinguishes **historical retention, resonant organization, reciprocal coupling, and recoverability** as separate observables whose relationships can be investigated.

The central physical candidate is not an isolated plasma layer, a single electromagnetic frequency, or an independently acting biological observer.

It is the evolving environmental configuration within which differentiated processes interact, historical states remain consequential, and changing physical conditions govern subsequent activity.

This configuration provides the physical setting for the mathematical formalism developed in Section 4.

There, the investigation proceeds from identified environmental relationships to explicit descriptions of composite signal formation, boundary-dependent transformations, state evolution, reciprocal interaction, and relational invariance.
